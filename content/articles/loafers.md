The loafer is the shoe for people who want to look put together without trying too hard. Slip it on, and it works with everything from chinos to a tailored suit.

## A brief history

The loafer's ancestry is often traced to traditional slip-on shoes worn in Norway and to the moccasins made by Indigenous peoples of North America. Visitors to Norway in the early 20th century reportedly admired local slip-ons, and versions began to be made for export.

Around the 1920s and 1930s, comfortable slip-ons appeared in Britain and the United States as country and leisure shoes. By the 1930s, American makers were producing moccasin-style loafers with a leather strap across the vamp, a design that would become enormously popular.

In the postwar decades, loafers became a fixture of the Ivy League look, worn by students with chinos and button-down shirts. Later, horsebit and tasseled versions brought the style into the boardroom, and today loafers span everything from soft suede drivers to polished dress shoes.

## Key features

1. **Moccasin apron stitching.** Many loafers feature a raised seam that forms a U-shaped apron over the top of the foot. This stitching reflects the shoe's moccasin roots, where a single piece of leather wraps up from beneath the foot. It gives the loafer its distinctive, slightly rustic look.
2. **Penny strap saddle.** The leather band across the vamp, called the saddle, often has a small slot cut into it. The style earned the nickname "penny loafer" by around the mid-century, when students supposedly tucked a coin into the slot.
3. **Low stacked heel.** Loafers usually sit on a short heel built from layers of leather stacked together. It provides a little lift and structure while keeping the shoe relaxed and easy to walk in.
4. **Laceless slip-on design.** With no laces or buckles, the loafer relies on a well-shaped opening and close fit to stay on. That makes it quick to wear and gives a clean, uncluttered line.
5. **Decorative details.** Variations include tassels, metal horsebit hardware and kiltie fringes over the vamp. These details change the character of the shoe, from preppy to more refined.

## Materials

Most loafers are made from smooth calf leather, which can be polished to a soft shine. Suede is a popular alternative for a softer, more casual look, especially in warmer months.

Soles are typically leather for dressier pairs, while rubber or rubber-studded soles add grip and durability for everyday wear. Driving loafers use very soft leather with rubber nubs on the sole and are best kept for light use.

## When and how to wear it

Loafers sit in a useful middle ground between casual and formal. Pair them with chinos, tailored trousers or dark jeans, and they work equally well with a knit polo, an oxford shirt or a blazer.

Dark, polished loafers can be worn with a suit in many offices, though classic lace-ups remain more conventional for the most formal occasions. Trousers with a slightly shorter, cleaner hem show off the shoe well. In summer, many people wear loafers with no-show socks for a bare-ankle look.

## Finding the right fit

Because loafers have no laces, fit is everything. They should feel snug across the instep and around the heel, with only minimal heel slip as you walk.

Leather loafers stretch slightly and mold to your foot over time, so a pair that feels a little firm at first is often right. Avoid pairs that pinch the toes or dig into the sides. If you are between sizes, try both and pay close attention to how secure your heel feels.

## Care and maintenance

Use cedar shoe trees after each wear to absorb moisture and help the shoes keep their shape. Rotate pairs so the leather has time to dry out.

Clean smooth leather with a soft brush, then apply cream polish and buff to a gentle shine. Treat suede with a suede brush and a protective spray, and keep it away from heavy rain. Have leather soles and heels repaired by a cobbler before they wear through to extend the life of the shoe.
