Brogues are the dress shoes with personality, instantly recognizable by their rows of tiny holes and decorative edges. Those perforations look purely ornamental today, but they have a practical backstory. Here is what the holes really mean and how to wear brogues well.

## A brief history

Brogues trace their roots to the rural shoes of Scotland and Ireland. The word comes from the Gaelic "bróg," which simply means shoe. Early versions were rough, sturdy footwear made from untanned hide for working outdoors in wet, boggy country.

The popular story is that the holes were punched through the leather so water could drain out after walkers crossed streams and marshes. Historians cannot confirm every detail, but the link between perforations and wet terrain is widely repeated.

For a long time, brogues were considered country shoes, unsuitable for town. That changed in the early twentieth century, when they became fashionable for golf, weekend wear, and eventually business dress. The perforations stayed, though by then they were decorative rather than functional.

## Key features

1. **Decorative perforations.** The rows of small holes, called broguing, run along the seams and edges of the leather pieces. Often they are paired with serrated, or pinked, edges. The more broguing a shoe has, the more casual it generally looks.
2. **Wingtip toe cap.** On a full brogue, the toe cap extends back along the sides in a curved "W" or "M" shape, resembling spread wings. This wingtip design is the most recognizable brogue style.
3. **Lacing.** Brogues can be built on either an Oxford or a Derby pattern. Closed-laced brogues look more refined, while open-laced versions feel more relaxed and suit wider or higher feet.
4. **Toe medallion.** Many brogues feature a decorative pattern of perforations punched into the center of the toe cap. It adds visual interest and marks the shoe as a more decorative style.
5. **Levels of broguing.** Quarter brogues have perforations along the toe cap only. Semi-brogues add a medallion, full brogues have the wingtip, and longwings carry the wing all the way around to the heel.

## Materials

Brogues are usually made from calfskin, with brown being by far the most traditional color. Tan, oxblood, and dark brown all show off the perforations nicely, and black brogues offer a slightly more formal option.

Suede brogues are a soft, relaxed alternative that works especially well in the fall. Two-tone brogues, combining white with brown or black, have a distinctly vintage feel. Soles range from thin leather for city wear to thicker leather or rubber for more rugged versions.

## When and how to wear it

Brogues sit in the middle of the formality scale. Quarter brogues and semi-brogues in dark leather can work with business suits, while full brogues and wingtips are better suited to tweed, flannel, and smart casual outfits.

Brown brogues are excellent with chinos, corduroys, and dark denim. They also pair well with knitwear and sport coats. Generally, the heavier and more textured your clothing, the more broguing your shoes can carry.

Avoid heavily brogued shoes for black tie or very formal occasions. A plain Oxford is the traditional choice there.

## Finding the right fit

Fit depends mainly on the underlying shoe pattern. Closed-laced brogues have less room to adjust over the instep, while open-laced versions are more forgiving. Try both if you are unsure which suits your foot.

The heel should feel secure with only slight lift as you walk. Leave a little space in front of your toes and check that the shoe bends where your foot naturally flexes. Leather will relax over time, but the shoe should feel comfortable from the start.

## Care and maintenance

Perforations collect dust and old polish, so brush brogues regularly with a soft brush that can reach into the holes. Apply cream polish sparingly, then buff well so no residue builds up in the broguing.

Use cedar shoe trees after each wear to absorb moisture and hold the shape. Let each pair rest a day between wears.

For suede brogues, use a suede brush and a protective spray. Resole stitched pairs when the soles wear thin, and your brogues can last many years.
