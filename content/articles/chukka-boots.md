Chukka and desert boots sit in a sweet spot between sneakers and dress shoes. They are simple, light, and easy to wear, yet they look more considered than a pair of trainers. If you want one boot that handles weekends and relaxed offices alike, this family is worth knowing.

## A brief history

The chukka's exact origins are debated. The name is usually linked to polo, where a "chukka" is a period of play, though players did not necessarily wear these boots on horseback. The style was likely worn off the field by players and spectators in the early twentieth century.

The desert boot is a close cousin with a clearer story. During the Second World War, British soldiers serving in North Africa are said to have worn lightweight suede boots bought from local bazaars. These boots were comfortable in hot, sandy conditions.

After the war, a version of this design was introduced to the wider public around 1950. Its crepe sole and soft suede made it a hit, and by the 1960s it had become a favorite of students, musicians, and style-conscious young people.

## Key features

1. **Two-eyelet lacing.** Chukkas typically have just two or three pairs of eyelets. This open, minimal lacing keeps the boot easy to slip on and gives it a clean, uncluttered look.
2. **Ankle-height shaft.** The boot ends right around the ankle, lower than most work or hiking boots. It offers a bit of coverage without the bulk of a taller boot.
3. **Crepe sole.** Desert boots in particular are known for soles made from natural crepe rubber. Crepe feels cushioned and springy underfoot, though it can pick up dirt and wear faster than harder rubbers.
4. **Open lacing construction.** The eyelet panels are stitched on top of the vamp rather than under it. This makes the boot more forgiving across the instep and adds to its casual character.
5. **Minimal upper.** Most chukkas are made from just two or three pieces of material. Fewer seams mean a smooth look and a quick break-in.

## Materials

Suede is the classic choice, especially in sandy, tan, and brown tones. It is soft from day one and gives the boot its relaxed personality. Smooth or pebbled leather versions look sharper and handle wet weather better.

Soles vary widely. Crepe offers comfort and that signature look, while leather soles create a dressier chukka that pairs well with tailoring. Rubber soles are the practical pick for rain and city streets.

Linings are often minimal or absent, which helps keep the boot light and breathable in warmer months.

## When and how to wear it

Chukkas are among the most adaptable boots around. Pair suede desert boots with chinos, a polo shirt, or an oxford shirt for a classic smart-casual look. They also sit comfortably with dark jeans and a light jacket.

Leather chukkas with leather soles can step up to wool trousers and a blazer. They are a good option for offices with a relaxed dress code.

They work well in spring and autumn. Crepe soles feel pleasant on dry pavements but can be slippery on wet surfaces, so save those pairs for drier days.

## Finding the right fit

Chukkas tend to have a roomy, simple shape, so they often fit close to your usual size. Check that your toes have a little space and that your heel stays put when you walk. With only two eyelets, the boot relies on the shape of the heel cup for stability.

Suede and unlined leather will soften quickly. A snug, comfortable fit at first will usually relax slightly with wear. Try them with the socks you would normally pair them with.

## Care and maintenance

Treat suede with a protective spray before the first wear and touch it up every few months. Brush regularly with a suede brush to lift the nap and remove dust. A suede eraser handles small marks.

Crepe soles collect grit, so give them a quick scrub with a stiff brush and a little warm water. Keep them away from direct heat, which can make crepe soft and sticky. Leather chukkas benefit from regular conditioning and shoe trees between wears.
