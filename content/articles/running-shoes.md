A good running shoe can make the difference between a run you enjoy and one you endure. With so many shapes, foams and labels on offer, choosing a pair can feel technical, but the basics are simple once you know what to look for.

## A brief history

People have run for as long as there have been people, but purpose-built running footwear is fairly recent. By the late 1800s, athletes in Britain were racing in leather shoes fitted with metal spikes for grip on grass and cinder tracks. Around the same time, rubber-soled canvas plimsolls offered a cheaper, lighter option for everyday exercise.

The big shift came with the jogging boom of the 1970s. As running moved from the track to the road, ordinary people wanted shoes that could soften thousands of strides on hard pavement. Designers responded with thicker foam midsoles, flared heels and lighter nylon uppers.

Since then, the running shoe has kept evolving. The past decade or so has brought springier foams, rocker-shaped soles and, for racing, stiff plates embedded in the midsole. At the same time, a minimalist movement pushed for thinner, flexible shoes, proving there is no single right answer.

## Key features

1. **Cushioned midsole.** The foam layer between the outsole and your foot does most of the work. It absorbs some of the impact of each landing and returns a little energy as you push off. Softer foams feel plush on long, easy runs, while firmer ones feel more stable and responsive at speed.
2. **Breathable mesh upper.** Feet heat up and sweat during a run, so most uppers use engineered or knit mesh that lets air move through. A well-made mesh also stretches slightly with your foot as it swells over longer distances.
3. **Heel counter.** This is the stiffened cup wrapped around the back of the shoe. It holds your heel in place and helps keep the foot from sliding or rolling. Some runners prefer a rigid counter, while others like a softer, more flexible one.
4. **Heel-to-toe drop.** Drop is the height difference between the heel and the forefoot, usually somewhere between zero and about 12 millimeters. A higher drop can ease strain on the calves and Achilles, while a lower drop encourages a more midfoot landing.
5. **Rubber outsole.** Strategically placed rubber pads protect the foam from wear and add grip. Road shoes use flatter patterns, while trail shoes add deeper lugs for loose dirt and mud.

## Materials

Most modern midsoles are made from EVA foam or newer blends based on other polymers, chosen for their balance of softness, bounce and weight. Outsoles are typically carbon or blown rubber, which trade durability against grip and lightness.

Uppers lean heavily on synthetic textiles such as polyester mesh and knit, sometimes reinforced with thin overlays rather than heavy stitched leather. Many shoes now also include recycled fibers in the upper and laces.

## When and how to wear it

Running shoes are built for running, and they perform best when kept mostly for that job. Using them for long days on your feet or in the gym wears out the foam faster and can change how they feel on a run.

That said, plenty of people wear an older pair casually with jeans, joggers or shorts. If you go that route, retire your former running pair to everyday use and keep a fresh one for training. For lifting weights, a flatter, firmer shoe is usually more stable than a soft running shoe.

## Finding the right fit

Shop later in the day, when your feet are slightly larger, and bring the socks you run in. Aim for roughly a thumb's width of space between your longest toe and the end of the shoe, since feet slide forward and swell during a run.

The heel should feel secure without rubbing, and the widest part of your foot should sit comfortably in the widest part of the shoe. Many models come in wide versions, which are worth trying if you feel pressure on the sides.

Above all, trust comfort. A short jog in the store or around your home will tell you more than any label. If a shoe feels wrong in the first few minutes, it rarely improves.

## Care and maintenance

Let wet or sweaty shoes air dry at room temperature, away from radiators and direct heat, which can warp foam and weaken glue. Stuffing them with newspaper helps draw out moisture.

Clean dirt off with a soft brush and mild soapy water rather than the washing machine. Rotating two pairs gives the foam time to recover between runs. Most shoes last somewhere around 300 to 500 miles, so replace them when the cushioning feels flat or the outsole is worn smooth.
