Head out on a trail and you will see both low hiking shoes and taller hiking boots. Each has its place, and the right choice depends on the terrain, the weight on your back and how your feet and ankles like to move.

## A brief history

For centuries, people walking in the mountains wore heavy leather boots, often studded with iron nails for grip on rock and ice. By the late 1800s and early 1900s, as mountaineering grew popular in the Alps, these nailed boots were standard equipment.

A major turning point came in the 1930s, when molded rubber soles with deep lugs were developed in Italy. They gripped well, lasted longer and were far lighter than nailed soles, and they gradually became the norm after the Second World War.

From around the 1970s and 1980s, lighter fabric-and-leather boots appeared, borrowing ideas from running shoes. Waterproof breathable membranes followed, and today's hikers can choose from sturdy backpacking boots, lighter mid-height boots and nimble low-cut hiking shoes.

## Key features

1. **Deep lugged outsole.** Chunky lugs bite into dirt, mud and loose gravel, while widely spaced patterns help shed mud. Lugs at the heel are often angled to help with braking on descents. Stickier rubber grips better on rock but tends to wear faster.
2. **Protective toe bumper.** A rubber cap wraps the front of the shoe to shield your toes from rocks and roots. It also protects the upper from scuffs and abrasion on rough ground.
3. **Padded collar.** Cushioning around the ankle opening keeps the shoe comfortable and helps seal out grit. On boots, the higher padded collar also wraps the ankle for a more secure, supported feel on uneven trails.
4. **Supportive midsole.** Hiking footwear usually has a firmer midsole than a running shoe, sometimes with a stiff shank or plate. This spreads pressure from sharp stones and adds stability under a heavy pack.
5. **Waterproof membrane.** Many models include a breathable waterproof liner to keep feet dry in wet grass and shallow streams. The trade-off is that they can feel warmer and take longer to dry if water gets in over the top.

## Materials

Traditional boots use full-grain leather, which is tough, water-resistant and molds to the foot, though it is heavier and needs breaking in. Lighter designs combine suede or nubuck leather with nylon mesh to save weight and improve breathability.

Outsoles are made from rubber compounds tuned for grip and durability. Midsoles are usually EVA or polyurethane foam, with polyurethane being denser and longer-lasting under heavy loads.

## When and how to wear it

Hiking shoes suit well-maintained trails, day hikes and warm weather. They are lighter, more flexible and need little breaking in, making them a good choice for fast walkers.

Hiking boots come into their own on rocky, rough or wet terrain, on multi-day trips and when carrying a heavy pack. The extra height and stiffness can help on long descents and loose ground. Pair either with merino or synthetic hiking socks rather than cotton, which holds moisture and can lead to blisters.

## Finding the right fit

Try on hiking footwear in the afternoon, wearing the socks you hike in. There should be room to wiggle your toes and roughly a finger's width in front of them, so your toes do not hit the end on descents.

Your heel should stay put as you walk, with only slight lift. Many outdoor stores have a small ramp, which is useful for checking whether your foot slides forward going downhill. Break new boots in gradually with short walks before taking them on a long hike.

## Care and maintenance

After each hike, knock off mud and rinse dirt away with clean water and a soft brush. Remove the insoles and let everything dry at room temperature, away from fires and heaters, which can crack leather and weaken adhesives.

Reproof the outer fabric or leather now and then with a suitable treatment so water beads off rather than soaking in. Condition full-grain leather occasionally to keep it supple, and check the outsoles for worn lugs, which reduce grip on slippery ground.
