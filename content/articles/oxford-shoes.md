If you own only one pair of dress shoes, it should probably be an Oxford. Clean, sleek, and quietly confident, it is the shoe that suits job interviews, weddings, and formal dinners alike. Knowing how it is built makes it much easier to choose a good one.

## A brief history

The Oxford's name is usually linked to Oxford University in England. The common account is that students in the early nineteenth century began favoring a laced half-boot, sometimes called the Oxonian, over the tall boots that were standard at the time. Over the following decades the style got lower and lower until it became the shoe we recognize.

By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the low-cut, closed-laced Oxford had become a staple of men's formal and business dress. In some places it is also called a Balmoral, a name often connected with the Scottish castle of the same name.

Today the Oxford is worn by people of all genders, and it remains the benchmark against which other dress shoes are measured.

## Key features

1. **Closed lacing.** This is the defining detail. The eyelet tabs, called quarters, are stitched beneath the front section of the shoe, called the vamp, so they close neatly together when laced. The result is a smooth, close-fitting line across the top of the foot.
2. **Cap toe.** Many classic Oxfords have a separate piece of leather stitched across the toe, forming a straight line. The cap toe is perhaps the most traditional business style and works for nearly any formal occasion.
3. **Stacked leather heel.** The heel is built from layers of leather stacked and pressed together, then trimmed and finished. It gives a firm, low, refined base, and the layers are often visible along the side.
4. **Slim, sleek profile.** Oxfords tend to have a narrower silhouette and thinner sole than casual shoes. That trim shape is a big part of their formal look.
5. **Plain or subtle decoration.** The most formal Oxfords are plain or simply capped. Variations add perforations, a wholecut design from a single piece of leather, or a polished patent finish for black tie.

## Materials

Smooth calfskin is the classic choice, prized for its fine grain and its ability to take a high shine. Black and dark brown are the most versatile colors, with black being the most formal. Suede Oxfords exist too, but they read as more relaxed.

Quality Oxfords usually have leather soles, leather linings, and stitched rather than glued construction. Stitched shoes can often be resoled, which extends their life considerably. Some modern versions use rubber soles for grip in wet weather.

## When and how to wear it

Black cap-toe Oxfords are a safe choice for business suits, interviews, funerals, and formal events. Patent leather Oxfords are traditional with a tuxedo. Brown Oxfords are slightly less formal and pair well with navy, gray, and earth-toned suits.

Oxfords look best with tailored trousers that have a clean break at the ankle. They are less at home with jeans or shorts, where a Derby or loafer usually works better. Match your belt to your shoes for a polished finish.

## Finding the right fit

Because the lacing is closed, Oxfords have less room to adjust over the instep. If you have a high instep, the laces may not close fully, so try several styles. Ideally, the two sides of the lacing should sit roughly parallel with a small gap between them.

The heel should feel snug, with only slight lift as you walk. Leave a little space in front of your toes, and check that the widest part of your foot lines up with the widest part of the shoe. Leather will soften, but it should not pinch from the start.

## Care and maintenance

Use cedar shoe trees after every wear to absorb moisture and hold the shape. Brush off dust, then apply cream polish occasionally to feed the leather, followed by wax polish when you want more shine on the toe and heel.

Give each pair a day of rest between wears so the leather can dry out. When the sole or heel wears thin, have a cobbler replace it before damage reaches the upper.

Well-made Oxfords that are cared for properly can last many years, often looking better with age.
